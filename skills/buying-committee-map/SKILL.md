# Buying Committee Map

## When to use

- After discovery, when the buyer has agreed there is a problem and several people beyond your main contact will have a say.
- Any deal where you have only spoken to a single contact, or a very small group, on the buyer side.
- A deal has stalled and nobody can explain who is holding it up.
- Before a deal inspection, a forecast call, or a handover between sellers.
- A champion goes quiet, changes role, or leaves, and you need to know who else you can reach.
- Before a decision meeting, so every attendee has been heard and equipped in advance.

## What you need

Ask for these before drafting:

1. **Deal context:** the buyer company, the problem being solved, the decision date or event, and the current stage.
2. **Known people:** names, titles, team, and how each has engaged so far (met, emailed, mentioned by others, never seen).
3. **Suspected people:** roles you think exist but have not met, such as finance, security, legal, procurement, IT, the end users, or an executive sponsor.
4. **What you have heard:** quotes, objections, questions, and behaviour in meetings that hint at each person's view.
5. **The buying process as described:** who recommends, who approves, who signs.
6. **Your own coverage:** who on your side knows whom, and which relationships are warm or cold.
7. **Competing options:** other vendors, an internal build, or doing nothing, and who favours each.

If the seller can name only one person, still proceed. The empty rows are the most valuable part of the map.

## Process

1. **Start with the decision, not the org chart.** Write one sentence naming the decision being made and the event that forces it. Everyone on the map is judged by how they touch that decision, not by their title.
2. **List every person.** Include people you have met, people mentioned in passing, and placeholder rows for roles that must exist but are unnamed (for example, "whoever signs the contract"). Do not drop someone because they seem junior or distant; a person who can say no or slow things down belongs on the map.
3. **Tag each person with one primary role.** Use champion, evaluator, economic buyer, blocker, or unknown. Add a secondary tag only when truly needed. A champion is someone who wants you to win and will spend their own credibility on it. A friendly contact who gives information but takes no action is not yet a champion; tag them honestly.
4. **Record stance.** For each person, note whether they are supportive, neutral, opposed, or not known. Next to the stance, write the evidence in a few words: what they said or did. If there is no evidence, the stance is "not known", never a guess dressed as fact.
5. **Capture what each person cares about.** Write their personal and professional stake: the outcome they are measured on, the risk they fear, the work they would inherit, the budget they would lose. Note the open concern in their own words where possible.
6. **Mark the relationship strength.** Note who on your side owns the relationship, when it was last touched, and whether the contact would take your call. A strong stance with no live relationship is still a gap.
7. **Find the gaps.** Scan for these patterns: no economic buyer met, no one who uses the product, a blocker with no plan, a champion with no access above them, an evaluator who has not seen the product, roles tagged unknown, and a single seller owning every relationship.
8. **Pick one outreach action per gap.** Each action names the person, the channel, the message or asset, the owner on your side, and a date. Examples: ask the champion for an introduction with a specific reason, offer finance a short briefing built around their model, invite the technical evaluator to a working session, send the opposed stakeholder a direct answer to their stated concern. Prefer actions that give the person something useful, not a request for their time with no reason.
9. **Equip your champion.** For each person who will decide or influence, ask what the champion needs to represent you when you are not in the room: a short internal summary, answers to likely objections, a proof point relevant to that person's concern. Draft those assets as part of the plan.
10. **Plan for the blocker.** For each opposed or doubtful person, write what you think their real objection is, test it by asking the champion or the person directly, and decide whether to address it, route around it, or flag the risk in the forecast.
11. **Review before the decision meeting.** Walk the map and ask who will be in the room, who will not but will be consulted afterward, and whether anyone will hear about you for the first time that day. Close those gaps before the meeting or reset expectations about the date.
12. **Keep it alive.** Update the map after every meaningful interaction, and review it at every deal inspection. Change stance only when new evidence arrives, and keep a short log of what changed and why.

## Output format

```
Decision: [what is being decided, by when, and why then]

| Name | Title / team | Role | Stance | Evidence for stance | What they care about | Open concern | Relationship owner | Last contact | Next step | Next step date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Gaps found:
- [gap, why it matters]

Outreach plan:
1. [person] - [action] - [owner] - [date]

Champion kit:
- [asset or talking point, who it is for]

Risks and blockers:
- [person, suspected objection, plan]

Changes since last review:
- [what moved and the evidence]
```

Keep the table to one line of text per cell. If a cell would need a paragraph, the detail belongs in call notes, not on the map.

## Quality checklist

- The decision and its driving event are written at the top.
- Every row has a role tag, a stance, and evidence for that stance, or says "not known".
- Placeholder rows exist for roles you have not identified yet.
- An economic buyer is named, or the lack of one is listed as the top gap.
- Each person has an owner on your side and a dated next step.
- Each gap has one clear outreach action, not a vague intention to "engage".
- Blockers and doubters have a written plan, not just a label.
- The champion is tested: what have they actually done for you?
- The map is shareable inside your team and contains nothing you would be embarrassed for a colleague to read.
- A review date is set.

## Common mistakes

- **Mapping titles instead of influence.** A senior title does not mean real sway, and a quiet analyst may hold the deciding opinion. Ask who others defer to.
- **Calling a friendly contact a champion.** Warmth is not advocacy. If they have not acted on your behalf, mark them as an evaluator or unknown.
- **Guessing stance.** Silence is not support. Record what you observed, and mark the rest unknown.
- **Leaving blockers off the map.** Avoiding the uncomfortable rows does not remove the people.
- **One-off mapping.** A map built once and never reviewed is a snapshot of a deal that no longer exists.
- **Outreach with no reason.** Asking for a meeting without something relevant to offer burns goodwill. Tie each action to that person's stated concern.
- **Going around the champion without telling them.** Multi-threading should be agreed with your main contact, or you risk losing them.
- **Treating the map as a sales-only secret.** Colleagues, managers, and sometimes the champion should be able to see and correct it.
- **Too many next steps.** Pick the few actions that close the biggest gaps first.

## Example prompts

- "Build a buying committee map for a deal where I have only spoken to the head of operations. Here are my call notes."
- "Review this stakeholder table and tell me which roles are missing, which stances lack evidence, and what to do next."
- "My champion just moved to another team. Help me re-map the deal and decide who to reach first."
- "Draft outreach actions for each gap on this map, tied to what each person cares about."
- "Prepare a deal inspection summary from this map: top risks, unmet roles, and the next dated step for each person."
- "Write a short internal briefing my champion can use to bring the finance lead on board."
