# Case Study Writer

This skill produces customer stories that prospects believe. A good case study is about the customer, not the vendor: their situation, why they decided to change, how it went, and what is measurably different now. It also produces the shorter formats that sales teams actually use day to day.

## When to use

- A customer has achieved a result worth sharing.
- Sales needs proof for a specific segment, use case or objection.
- Interview notes or a call recording exist but no story has been written.
- An existing case study reads like a press release and nobody uses it.

## What you need

Ask for these first. If no interview has happened, start with the interview guide in step 1.

1. **Customer context:** industry, size, region, the team involved, and the roles of the people interviewed.
2. **Interview material:** notes, transcript or answers to the interview questions.
3. **Results:** metrics with a baseline and a time frame, plus qualitative outcomes.
4. **Permission level:** named with logo, named without logo, or anonymized ("a 400-person logistics company").
5. **Target reader:** the persona and segment this story should persuade.
6. **Use cases:** website, sales deck, outbound emails, proposals, social posts.

## Process

1. **Run or plan the interview.** If needed, provide a guide that covers:
   - Before: what was happening, what it cost them, what they had tried.
   - Trigger: why they decided to act at that moment.
   - Evaluation: options considered, concerns, why they chose this solution.
   - Implementation: how it went, surprises, effort required.
   - Results: what changed, with numbers and a time frame; what colleagues noticed.
   - Advice: what they would tell a peer considering the same move.
   Ask for specific moments and quotes, not general satisfaction.
2. **Find the story spine.** Identify the single most compelling change, ideally tied to a business outcome (revenue, cost, time, risk). Everything in the story should support it.
3. **Validate the numbers.** Each metric needs a baseline, a result and a period ("from 6 weeks to 9 days over two quarters"). Mark any number that is an estimate. Avoid attributing all improvement to the product if other factors were involved; honest attribution increases credibility.
4. **Choose the structure.** A dependable outline: headline with the outcome, summary box (customer, challenge, solution, results), the situation, the decision, the implementation, the results, a closing quote, and what is next for the customer.
5. **Write in the customer's voice where possible.** Use direct quotes for the emotional and evaluative parts. Keep vendor descriptions short and focused on what the customer used, not a feature tour.
6. **Handle objections inside the story.** If the target reader worries about implementation effort or switching risk, show how this customer handled it.
7. **De-identify properly when required.** Replace names with descriptive labels, generalize or remove distinctive details (exact location, unique product names, specific dates, unusual figures), and assess how easily a knowledgeable reader such as a competitor or industry insider could still recognize the customer from the combination. De-identification lowers that risk but cannot guarantee it, so the customer approves the final anonymized version explicitly, just as they would a named one.
8. **Create derivative formats.** One-paragraph summary, a three-line proof snippet for emails, a slide (challenge, solution, results, quote), and two social post angles.
9. **Prepare for approval.** List the quotes and numbers the customer must approve, and flag anything sensitive.

## Output format

```
Headline: [outcome-focused, specific]

At a glance
- Customer: [name or anonymized description]
- Challenge: [one line]
- Solution: [one line]
- Results: [2-3 bullet metrics with time frame]

The situation
[paragraph(s)]

The decision
[paragraph(s), why now, options considered]

Making it work
[paragraph(s), implementation and how risks were handled]

The results
[paragraph(s) with metrics and quotes]

"[closing quote]" [Name or role, company or description]

Short formats
- Summary paragraph: ...
- Email proof snippet (3 lines): ...
- Slide: Challenge / Solution / Results / Quote
- Social angles: 1) ... 2) ...

For customer approval: [list of quotes and figures]
```

## Quality checklist

- The customer is the protagonist; vendor description is brief.
- Every metric has a baseline, result and time frame, or is labeled as an estimate.
- At least two direct quotes, attributed appropriately for the permission level.
- The "why now" and alternatives considered are included.
- Anonymized stories have had distinctive details generalized, their re-identification risk is assessed, and the customer has approved the final text.
- Short formats are consistent with the full story.
- Approval items are listed.

## Common mistakes

- **Vendor as hero.** Stories that spend most words on product features read as ads and are less persuasive.
- **Results without context.** "Increased efficiency by 50 percent" means little without a baseline, a measure and a time frame.
- **Smoothing over difficulty.** A story with no challenges feels fabricated. A brief, honest hurdle builds trust.
- **Generic quotes.** "They are a great partner" proves nothing. Ask for quotes about specific changes.
- **Weak anonymization.** Removing the name but keeping a unique combination of city, size and niche identifies the customer anyway.
- **Only the long version.** Most proof is used in emails, slides and calls. Without short formats, the case study sits unused.

## Example prompts

- "Here's the transcript of our call with a customer. Write a case study and email snippets."
- "Create an interview guide for a customer who cut onboarding time by half."
- "Anonymize this case study so the customer can't be identified, but keep it credible."
- "Turn this case study into a sales slide and two LinkedIn posts."
- "Our case studies read like press releases. Rewrite this one around the customer's story."
