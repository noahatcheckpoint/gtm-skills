# Positioning Statement

This skill produces positioning that explains, in terms a buyer already understands, why your offer is the better choice for a specific kind of customer. The output is a short positioning statement plus the reasoning underneath it, so the team can defend every word.

## When to use

- Launching a product, a new tier, or entering a new segment.
- Prospects keep comparing you to the wrong competitors, or asking "so what is it exactly?"
- The homepage headline could belong to any company in the space.
- Sales and marketing describe the product in different ways.
- Win rates are fine with one segment and poor with another, and nobody knows why.

## What you need

Ask for what is missing before you start. Positioning built without customer evidence is a guess and should be labeled as one.

1. **The offer:** what it is, how it is sold, rough price point.
2. **Your happiest customers:** who they are and, ideally, their own words for why they chose you.
3. **Deals you lost:** to whom, and the reasons given.
4. **What buyers did before you:** spreadsheets, an agency, an incumbent platform, hiring a person, doing nothing.
5. **Capabilities you believe are distinctive:** features, delivery model, expertise, data, pricing structure.
6. **Current messaging:** homepage hero, pitch deck opening, or elevator pitch.

## Process

1. **List the real alternatives.** Ask: if we disappeared tomorrow, what would our best customers do instead? Include non-product options ("keep using spreadsheets", "hire a contractor", "live with the problem"). Status quo is usually the biggest competitor and the one most teams forget.
2. **Isolate what is genuinely different.** For each alternative, list what you have or do that it lacks. Strip out table stakes (things every serious option offers). Keep only attributes a customer would notice and could verify.
3. **Translate attributes into value.** For every differentiator, answer "so what?" until you reach an outcome the buyer cares about: time saved, risk removed, revenue gained, a job done that could not be done before. Then attach proof: a metric, a customer quote, a demo moment, a guarantee.
4. **Find who cares most.** Identify the customers for whom that value matters most. They are usually a narrower group than your full customer base. Describe them by the situation that makes the value urgent, not just by size or industry.
5. **Choose the market frame.** Pick the category or reference point that makes your value obvious within seconds. Three options:
   - *Existing category, win head on:* works if you are clearly better on what the category already values.
   - *Subcategory:* an existing category plus a qualifier ("X for regulated teams") when you beat the leader for a specific segment.
   - *New category:* only when no existing frame fits and you have the budget and patience to educate a market. Treat this as the expensive option.
   A useful test: once the buyer hears the category, do they correctly assume your competitors, your price range and your must-have features?
6. **Add a trend if, and only if, it is real.** A relevant shift (regulation, a technology change, a buying behavior change) can explain "why now", but it should support the positioning, not replace it.
7. **Write the statement.** Use the template below. Then write a 10-word version a rep can say out loud.
8. **Stress test.** Swap your company name for your top competitor's. If the statement still reads true, it is not differentiated. Rewrite until it fails that test.

## Output format

```
Positioning summary

For [target customer in a specific situation]
who [struggle or need, in their words],
[product] is a [market frame]
that [primary value, stated as an outcome].
Unlike [main alternative, including status quo],
we [key differentiator that delivers the value].

Spoken version (10 words or fewer): ...

Alternatives map
| Alternative | Why buyers choose it | Where it falls short | Our differentiator |

Value and proof
| Differentiator | Value to the buyer | Proof we can show |

Best-fit customers: [situation-based description]
Market frame chosen: [category] because [reason]; rejected: [option] because [reason]
Why now (optional): [trend]
Competitor swap test: [pass/fail and what you changed]
Assumptions to validate with customers: ...
```

## Quality checklist

- Status quo or "do nothing" appears in the alternatives list.
- Every differentiator has a value statement and at least one piece of proof, or is flagged as unproven.
- The target customer is described by situation, not only firmographics.
- The category choice is explained, including what was rejected.
- The statement fails the competitor swap test.
- No superlatives ("best", "leading", "world-class") without proof attached.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the category you were built in.** Founders often default to the frame they had in mind on day one. The best frame is the one where your strengths matter most to buyers today.
- **Listing features as differentiators.** "AI-powered" or "easy to use" describes a property, not a reason to buy. Push through to the outcome.
- **Positioning against the competitor you fear rather than the one you meet.** Use loss data, not market maps.
- **Trying to appeal to everyone.** A broad target hides your strongest value. Narrow positioning tends to win more deals, including some outside the target.
- **Confusing positioning with messaging.** Positioning is the strategic choice; taglines and headlines come after and should be derived from it.
- **Inventing a category to sound visionary.** Category creation is slow and costly. Default to an existing frame unless the evidence forces you out of it.

## Example prompts

- "Here are notes from 8 customer interviews and our last 10 losses. Draft our positioning."
- "Our homepage says we're an 'all-in-one revenue platform'. Help us position more sharply."
- "Should we position as a subcategory of CRM or as something new? Walk me through it."
- "Write a positioning statement for our consulting offer and run the competitor swap test."
- "We sell to both startups and enterprises. Help us pick which one to position for first."
