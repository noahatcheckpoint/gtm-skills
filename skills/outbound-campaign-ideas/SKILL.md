# Outbound Campaign Ideas

This skill generates outbound campaign concepts and ranks them. A good campaign is a combination of three things: a narrow audience, a reason to reach out now, and an offer that is easier to say yes to than a sales call. The skill makes all three explicit for every idea.

## When to use

- The team keeps running one generic campaign to its whole list.
- Reply rates are dropping and the messaging feels exhausted.
- Planning next quarter's outbound and looking for a portfolio of plays.
- A new product, case study or event creates an opportunity to reach out.

## What you need

Ask for these before ideating:

1. **ICP and personas:** who you sell to and the roles involved.
2. **Offer and differentiators:** what you sell and why it wins.
3. **Assets:** case studies, data, benchmarks, events, tools, content, a free audit you could run.
4. **Data you can access:** what signals you can actually detect (hiring, funding, technology used, job changes, website visits, review activity, public filings).
5. **Capacity:** how many reps or sends per week, and whether campaigns can be manual and tailored or must scale.
6. **What has been tried:** past campaigns and how they performed.

## Process

1. **Generate across idea families.** Produce at least two ideas in each family so the list is not lopsided:
   - *Trigger campaigns:* reach out because something changed (new leader, funding, expansion, tool migration, regulation deadline).
   - *Peer proof campaigns:* "companies like yours" built around one case study in one segment.
   - *Insight campaigns:* share original data or a benchmark the audience cannot easily get.
   - *Free value campaigns:* a short audit, teardown, or tailored recommendation delivered before any ask.
   - *Event campaigns:* before, during and after a conference, webinar or local meetup.
   - *Competitor or status quo campaigns:* aimed at users of a specific alternative facing a known limitation (stay factual, never disparaging).
   - *Re-engagement campaigns:* closed-lost deals, churned customers, old inbound leads, past champions who changed jobs.
2. **Specify each idea.** For every idea define: audience (narrow enough to describe in one line), trigger or reason now, offer (what they get for replying), proof, channel mix, and the first-line hook.
3. **Pressure-test the offer.** Ask: would the recipient find the offer useful even if they never bought? If not, the offer is a pitch in disguise. Lower-commitment offers (a benchmark, a 2-page audit, a peer roundtable seat) usually outperform demo requests for cold audiences.
4. **Score the ideas.** Rate each 1 to 5 on: audience size (enough accounts to matter), signal reliability (can you actually detect the trigger), relevance (how urgent the problem is when the trigger fires), effort (inverse: 5 means easy), and proof strength. Sum the scores.
5. **Balance the portfolio.** Pick three to five ideas: at least one quick win (low effort, available data), one scalable always-on play, and one higher-effort, high-value play for top accounts.
6. **Brief the winners.** For each selected idea write a one-page brief a copywriter or rep can execute without a meeting.
7. **Define success.** Set a target positive reply rate or meeting count per campaign and a minimum sample size before judging (heuristic: at least a few hundred contacts for scaled campaigns, or 20 to 30 accounts for highly tailored ones).

```

Campaign brief: [name]
- Audience: ...
- Trigger / reason now: ...
- How to find them: ...
- Offer: ...
- Proof: ...
- Channels and cadence: ...
- Hook (first line): ...
- Success metric and sample size: ...
- Risks: ...
```

## Quality checklist

- Every idea names a specific audience, reason now and offer.
- At least five idea families are represented in the long list.
- Triggers are things the team can actually detect with the data it has.
- Offers have standalone value; not every offer is a demo.
- Ideas are scored and the scoring is visible.
- The portfolio mixes quick wins with longer plays.
- Briefs include success metrics and a minimum sample size.

## Common mistakes

- **Ideas that are really audiences.** "Target CFOs" is a segment, not a campaign. A campaign needs a reason and an offer.
- **Triggers you cannot detect.** A brilliant trigger with no data source becomes a manual research project that never ships.
- **Every offer is a meeting.** For cold prospects, a meeting is a big ask. Use offers that earn the meeting.
- **Disparaging competitors.** Competitive campaigns should highlight a factual limitation or a switching moment, not attack.
- **Judging too early.** Calling a campaign dead after 50 sends confuses noise with signal.
- **Ignoring re-engagement.** Past opportunities, churned customers and job-changing champions are often the warmest audiences available.

## Example prompts

- "Give me 15 outbound campaign ideas for our payroll software aimed at companies hiring abroad."
- "We have a strong case study in logistics. Build three campaigns around it."
- "Score these campaign ideas and recommend which three to run next quarter."
- "Our only campaign is 'book a demo'. What else could we offer cold prospects?"
- "Design a campaign around the trade show we're attending next month."
