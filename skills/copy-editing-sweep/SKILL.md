# Copy Editing Sweep

This skill improves copy that already exists. It works in separate passes, each looking for one kind of problem, which catches far more than a single read-through. The author's message and intent stay intact; the edit makes them sharper.

## When to use

- A draft page, email, post, one-pager or deck needs polishing before publishing.
- Copy feels long, vague or generic, but the structure is roughly right.
- Several people wrote parts of a document and the voice is uneven.
- A piece needs to sound less automated and more like a person wrote it.

If the copy has the wrong strategy (wrong audience, wrong offer), say so and recommend a rewrite instead of polishing.

## What you need

Ask for these first:

1. **The copy** in full.
2. **Purpose and audience:** what the piece should make the reader think or do.
3. **Format and constraints:** channel, word limits, character limits, required terms or legal lines.
4. **Voice guidance:** brand voice notes or an example of copy the team considers on-voice.
5. **Edit depth:** light (polish only), medium (tighten and clarify), or heavy (restructure allowed).

## Process

Run these passes in order. Later passes assume earlier fixes are done.

1. **Purpose pass.** Read the whole piece once. Write one sentence stating what it is trying to achieve. If any section does not serve that sentence, flag it for cutting or moving.
2. **Clarity pass.** Find sentences a reader would have to read twice. Split long sentences, put the subject and verb early, replace abstract nouns with verbs ("make a decision" becomes "decide"), and define or remove jargon.
3. **Specificity pass.** Hunt vague words: "solutions", "streamline", "robust", "seamless", "various", "significant". Replace each with a concrete detail (what exactly, how much, for whom) or cut it. If the specific fact is not available, mark a placeholder like [specific result needed] instead of inventing one.
4. **Reader pass.** Shift focus from the company to the reader. Count "we/our" versus "you/your" in key sections. Turn features into outcomes where it helps the reader understand why something matters.
5. **Proof pass.** Underline every claim. Each needs support nearby (a number, an example, a quote) or softer wording. Remove superlatives that cannot be backed up.
6. **Voice pass.** Align tone with the guidance: contractions or not, formality, sentence rhythm. Remove patterns that make text sound machine-written: stacked adjectives, "not only X but also Y" constructions repeated, lists of exactly three everywhere, empty openers like "In today's fast-paced world", and filler transitions.
7. **Structure pass.** Check headings, paragraph length and order. Headings should carry meaning when skimmed. Put the most important point first in each section.
8. **Polish pass.** Grammar, spelling, punctuation, consistent capitalization, number formatting, product name consistency, and broken parallelism in lists. Check any calls to action say what happens next.
9. **Length check.** Check the final length against the channel's real constraint (character limits, the space a layout allows, how long the reader has) and against what the reader needs to act. Report the change from the original as an observation, never a target: a sweep that removes padding may cut a lot, while one that adds missing proof or a clear next step may make copy longer.

## Output format

```
Edited copy
[full edited text]

Change log (significant edits only)
| # | Original | Edited | Pass | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Flags for the author
- Claims that need proof: ...
- Placeholders inserted: ...
- Sections recommended for cutting or moving: ...
- Strategic concerns (if any): ...

Length: [original words] -> [edited words]
```

## Quality checklist

- The edited copy still says what the author meant; no new claims were invented.
- Every vague term was made specific, cut, or flagged with a placeholder.
- Claims without proof are softened or flagged.
- Voice matches the guidance consistently from start to finish.
- The change log explains significant edits so the author can accept or reject them.
- Constraints (length, required terms, legal text) are respected.

## Common mistakes

- **Rewriting instead of editing.** Replacing the author's voice with your own makes the edit hard to accept and loses context only the author had.
- **Inventing specifics.** Making up a percentage to replace "significant" is worse than the vague word. Use placeholders.
- **One pass for everything.** Reading once for all problems misses most of them; focused passes are faster overall.
- **Polishing a strategic problem.** Beautiful sentences aimed at the wrong audience still fail. Flag it.
- **Over-trimming.** Cutting every transition can make copy choppy. Brevity serves clarity, not the reverse.
- **No explanation.** Edits without reasons are harder to learn from and easier to reject.

## Example prompts

- "Edit this landing page copy for clarity and specificity. Keep the structure."
- "Tighten this cold email to under 90 words without losing the proof point."
- "This blog post sounds AI-written. Edit it so it sounds like an experienced practitioner."
- "Do a light polish on this one-pager and flag any claims we can't back up."
- "Three people wrote this proposal. Make the voice consistent and give me a change log."
